This article details the creation of **FixSlidePic**, a web application developed to help a medical student convert low-quality, skewed lecture hall photos into clean, professional 16:9 PDF slide decks. The project serves as a case study for building robust agentic workflows using the **ADK (Agent Development Kit) for Java**.

### The Problem
Medical school professors frequently use complex visual data (e.g., molecular structures, fluorescence microscopy) on slides that are not shared with students. Photos taken in amphitheaters are typically plagued by:
*   **Geometric distortion:** Skewed angles and 3D perspective.
*   **Visual noise:** Amphitheater furniture, audience heads, stage lighting, and projected live-captioning bars.
*   **Lighting issues:** Washed-out contrast and "muddy" colors due to projector glare.
*   **Critical accuracy errors:** Minor hallucinations in generative models—such as mislabeling a carbon atom in a chemical ring—can render study materials dangerous for medical exams.

### Technical Solution: The Two-Tiered Approach
The author implemented a dual-layered strategy to ensure high-fidelity outputs:

#### 1. Offline Prompt Benchmarking
Before deploying, the author tested four prompt variations against 29 photos, including five "torture-test" images (e.g., slides with dark-mode themes, curved screens, and foreground obstructions). 
*   **Key Discovery:** Simple prompting led to systematic failures, such as forcing dark-mode slides to become white or mistaking subtitle bars for slide content.
*   **The Winner:** The final "v4_calibrated_master" prompt included five strict rules focused on isolating the canvas, rectifying geometry, purifying colors, inpainting occlusions, and maintaining verbatim text/diagram fidelity.

#### 2. Runtime Self-Correction (LLM-as-a-Judge)
To catch stochastic errors (one-off mistakes like a single misnumbered atom), the author implemented an **ADK `LoopAgent`** that runs up to three iterations:
*   **The Fixer:** Uses *Nano Banana 2.1* to generate the slide.
*   **The Judge:** Uses *Gemini 3.8 Flash* to compare the original photo against the result.
*   **Self-Correction:** If the Judge detects an error, it issues specific "corrective instructions." Crucially, the system **grounds the retry on the original photo** rather than the previous iteration to prevent "generation drift" (accumulating artifacts over multiple passes).
*   **Efficiency:** The system uses an `escalate(true)` trigger to exit the loop early the moment the output satisfies the rubric, saving time and compute resources.

### Application Architecture
*   **Backend:** Micronaut 5.2.5 (Java 25) with Apache PDFBox for PDF generation.
*   **Frontend:** A Progressive Web App (PWA) with multi-file drag-and-drop.
*   **UX Features:** The app provides real-time Server-Sent Events (SSE) to update the user on the restoration progress, a filmstrip view for batch management, and manual override buttons to allow users to verify or re-run specific slides.

### Key Conclusions
The author emphasizes that agentic workflows require a mix of **disciplined benchmarking** and **runtime verification**. 
1.  **Benchmarking** catches systematic flaws in instructions.
2.  **Runtime judging** catches the unpredictable "hallucinations" common in generative image tasks.
3.  **Calibration is vital:** Without a well-defined rubric (explaining what is "good enough" vs. what is a "critical error"), an LLM judge will be too nitpicky, leading to unnecessary re-runs and increased latency.
